# Part VII — Five marks over a characteristic-zero function field

**PROVED (scope of the result).** For every fixed $\delta>0$ there is one proper exceptional set across all degrees if the four ratios have presentations as quotients of polynomials with a bounded number of terms. The coefficients, exponents, roots of the parent, and degree of the common divisor in the presentations may all vary. A separate obstruction applies to arbitrary polynomials when most roots of the parent lie on a circle. I do not know how to deduce either restriction for arbitrary qualifying tuples. I could not decide the unrestricted question for **any** $0<\delta<1/10$, including $\delta<1/30$.

**PROVED (sharpness of the comparison).** There are primitive five-mark families over $\mathbb Q(i)[t]$ with $D=8n+1$, contact $h=4n$, and Zariski-dense ratios for each $n$. This attains the largest integral contact possible for a dominating family in these degrees. For four marks the polynomial Pell construction reaches every $\delta<1/6$. For three marks the endpoint $\delta=1/6$ itself is attained densely. The strict inequality $h>D/2$ still separates the five-point question from the new Pell examples.

## VII.1. Height, contact, and the exceptional-set quantifier

**PROVED.** Work first over $\mathbb C$. Write $q_1=1, q_j=z_j/z_1$ and $T=(\mathbb G_m)^4$. For a nonconstant map $f=[q_1:\cdots:q_5]$ on $\mathbb P^1$, let $d_+$ and $d_-$ denote the projective degrees of this map and its coordinatewise reciprocal. Put $W=d_++d_-$. Here the degree of a parametrized map includes its covering degree. At each place $v$, write $a_{iv}=v(q_i)$. Then
\[
 W=\sum_v(\max_i a_{iv}-\min_i a_{iv}).
 \tag{VII.1}
\]
Indeed the two projective heights are respectively the sums of $-\min_i a_{iv}$ and $\max_i a_{iv}$. For the conjugate-norm model with common polynomial gcd removed, the five degree-$D$ polynomials give a basepoint-free homogeneous presentation of degree $D$, and the conjugates give one for the reciprocal. Thus $W=2D$. In the split model (VII.1) specifies the height without any convention on common factors. The contact is
\[
 h=\min_{j>1}\operatorname{ord}_\infty(q_j-1)
   =D-\max_{i<j}\deg(z_i-z_j).
\]
The question is $h\ge(1/2+\delta)D$, or $h\ge(1/4+\delta/2)W$.

**PROVED.** I use a single proper closed subset of the **constant** torus that contains the images in $T$ of all qualifying maps of sufficiently large height. Its scalar extension gives an exceptional set over the function field. Conversely, for the full problem, allowing coefficients in $\mathbb C(t)$ does not weaken the non-density assertion. Clear a nonzero equation to $P(t,Y)$. For any qualifying $q$, the translates $q(t+s)$ qualify for all real constants $s$. Fix real $t_0$ for which $P(t_0,Y)\ne0$. Specializing and varying $s$ shows that $P(t_0,Y)$ vanishes on the whole image of $q$. Also, $t\mapsto t^k$ multiplies $W,h$ by $k$ without changing the image. Thus deleting finitely many degrees cannot remove one otherwise offending image. This convention admits a Zariski-dense countable union of curves. Excluding dominance for each fixed degree does not settle it.

**PROVED (the covering-family inequality used below).** Take a finite-type characteristic-zero family of rational maps with $W\le W_0$, all equal to $e$ modulo $t^{-h_0}$ at infinity. If the evaluation map dominates $T$, then
\[
                         4h_0+1\le W_0.                       \tag{VII.2}
\]
Here is a proof also covering split maps with arbitrary multiplicities. Work on a dominating irreducible component over its parameter function field, extending it finitely to split the finite zeros and poles of the generic coordinates. The source derivative and three parameter derivatives can be chosen with independent logarithmic derivative vectors at the generic point. Indeed the source derivative is nonzero for a nonconstant generic map, all derivatives together span the four-space by generic smoothness, and the parameter derivations extend over the separable algebraic extension. A component of source-constant maps has image $e$. At a finite place $t=a$, write $q_i=(t-a)^{m_i}u_i$ with $u_i$ a unit. The polar parts of **all four** vectors are scalar multiples of $(m_i)_{i=2}^5/(t-a)$. Their determinant has at most a simple pole, and only where (VII.1) charges at least one. At infinity the parameter vectors are $O(t^{-h_0})$ and the source vector is $O(t^{-h_0-1})$. A nonzero rational function with that zero needs at least $4h_0+1$ poles counted with multiplicity. This proves (VII.2). This is the fixed-family argument of round 29, with the two projective degrees kept separate. [Round 29, Theorem D](/home/user/Jarnik/docs/claude_review/round29/ff.md)

## VII.2. A uniform theorem for quotients of sparse polynomials

**PROVED.** Fix $b\ge1$ and $\epsilon>0$. There are a proper closed $Z_{b,\epsilon}\subset T$, definable over $\mathbb Q$, and $W_0$ with the following property. Suppose each $q_j\in\mathbb C(t)^*$ is a quotient of two Laurent polynomials with at most $b$ monomials **each**, and $q_j(\infty)=1$. If $W\ge W_0$ and $h\ge(1/4+\epsilon)W$, the whole ratio curve is in $Z_{b,\epsilon}$. The presentations may be chosen separately for the four coordinates, before cancellation of arbitrarily large common factors. A common affine change of source coordinate is allowed since it preserves the image, height and contact. In particular the result includes products of boundedly many binomials and their quotients, with arbitrary powers of the source variable in the binomials. There is no bound on the number or total multiplicity of places of the primitive parent. For a genuine tuple take $\epsilon=\delta/2$.

**PROVED (the gcd estimate).** Let $n=n_l\in\mathbb Z^r$ be a sequence such that $n\cdot u\ne0$ eventually for every fixed nonzero $u\in\mathbb Z^r$, and $L=\|n\|_\infty\to\infty$. For each $l$, let $F_1,\ldots,F_k$ be Laurent polynomials with exponents in a fixed box and with no common nonunit factor in $\mathbb C[Z_1^{\pm1},\ldots,Z_r^{\pm1}]$. Coefficients may depend on $l$. The total common vanishing, with multiplicities, of
\[
                    F_1(t^n),\ldots,F_k(t^n)\quad(t\in\mathbb C^*)
\]
is $o(L)$. We use $t^n=(t^{n_1},\ldots,t^{n_r})$. If $r=1$, substitution by a power map preserves the absence of a common torus zero, which proves the assertion directly.

For $r\ge2$, fix an integer $Q\ge2$. Simultaneous Dirichlet approximation applied to $n/L$, one of whose entries is $\pm1$, supplies $1\le b'\le Q^{r-1}$ and $v\in\mathbb Z^r$ such that
\[
      \|v-b'n/L\|_\infty\le 1/Q,\qquad \|v\|_\infty\le b'+1 .
\]
To see this, place the fractional parts of the other $r-1$ entries of $j n/L$, $0\le j\le Q^{r-1}$, in boxes of side $1/Q$ and subtract two in the same box. Take two generic constant linear combinations $A,C$ of the list with the full union of its supports and no common nonunit factor. After choosing $A$, one can avoid every irreducible factor of $A$ in $C$, because none divides the whole list. Substitute $Z_jX^{v_j}$ for $Z_j$ and divide both combinations by $X^a$, where $a$ is their minimum $v$-weight. The resulting polynomials in $X$ have the same degree $d=O(b')$. They are relatively prime over $\mathbb C(Z)[X]$. The substitution is an automorphism after adjoining $X^{\pm1}$, relative primeness persists under localization, and neither stripped polynomial is divisible by $X$.

For $d>0$ take their resultant $R(Z)\ne0$. The Sylvester identity, evaluated at $X=1$, puts it in the ideal $(A,C)$ in the Laurent ring. The determinant shows its support lies in a box of size $O(b')$, with the implied constant depending only on the original box and $r$. To see that all terms have the same $v$-weight, observe that replacing $Z$ by $ZS^v$ replaces each stripped polynomial $P(X)$ by $S^a P(SX)$, so the resultant scales by a single power of $S$. For $d=0$ use $R=A$, which has the same needed properties. For fixed $Q$ the allowed boxes are bounded, so eventually all distinct monomials in any such $R$ have distinct $n$-weights. Hence $R(t^n)\ne0$, regardless of its coefficients. If $u,u'$ are exponents of $R$, then
\[
 |n\cdot(u-u')|
   = |(n-Lv/b')\cdot(u-u')|\ll L/Q.
\]
Thus the total zero degree on $\mathbb C^*$ of $R(t^n)$ is $O(L/Q)$. The ideal membership bounds the common vanishing of **the whole list with its multiplicities** by this number. The implied constant is independent of $Q$, so we can let $Q\to\infty$. The argument is in the setting of sparse polynomial specialization. It only requires the estimate just proved and allows the complex coefficients to vary. [Amoroso, Leroux and Sombra on overdetermined lacunary systems](https://eaca2012.web.uah.es/textos/ponentes/amoroso.pdf)

**PROVED (passage to a fixed torus).** Suppose the theorem fails even with a $\mathbb Q$-defined exceptional set. Enumerate the nonzero Laurent polynomials over $\mathbb Q$. Choose maps of increasing $W\to\infty$ whose images escape the union of the first $l$ zero sets at step $l$. Write every distinct exponent in their four presentations in an integer vector $n_l$ of bounded length. Pass to a subsequence of fixed length and combinatorial placement. The ratios now come from rational functions in as many independent variables $Z_j$, by $Z_j=t^{n_{l,j}}$, with fixed support bounds. By a diagonal subsequence, every fixed integer vector pairs either eventually always to zero with $n_l$, or eventually never to zero. The former vectors form a saturated subgroup. Quotient the character lattice by it, choosing an integral basis for the free quotient. Its relations hold simultaneously on a tail by finite generation. In these coordinates every fixed bounded set of distinct monomials specializes injectively in exponent on a tail.

In the Laurent ring on this torus write projective coordinate lists $F=(F_i)$ for the five entries $1,q_2,\ldots,q_5$ formally, and $G=(G_i)$ for their reciprocals. Clear the bounded number of denominators and divide out the full Laurent gcd **separately in each list**. These operations take place after the lattice quotient and at each term of the sequence. Both resulting lists have uniformly bounded support boxes, up to a common monomial per list. Indeed the initial coordinate changes are fixed integer maps, and polynomial factors of a bounded multidegree polynomial have bounded degree in each variable after monomials are removed. The identities $F_iG_i=F_1G_1$ hold formally. After passing to a subsequence, all individual supports, and the supports of $F_i-F_1$, are fixed. Simultaneous multiplication of the $F_i$ by a single monomial has no effect on this bookkeeping.

For precision about unused large powers, take the subgroup generated by differences of support exponents **anywhere within each projective list**, including between two different entries of that list. Subtract one reference exponent per list and use a basis of this subgroup. This expresses both lists over the smaller Laurent ring, still with no common nonunit factor in either list, since such a factor would remain a nonunit upstairs. Use $n$ for the induced weight vector on the new basis. Write $d_F,d_G$ for the widths (maximum minus minimum) of the $n$-weights across the respective lists. Each basis element is a fixed integral combination of support differences, so
\[
        \|n\|_\infty\ll d_F+d_G\ll\|n\|_\infty .
\]
All fixed supports remain exponent-injective eventually. The nonzero primitive height rules out rank zero and makes these widths unbounded along the sequence. Clearing the lowest power of $t$ makes the specialized $F$-list a projective presentation of raw degree $d_F$, with no common zero at $0$ or $\infty$. Its degree loss is exactly its common vanishing on $\mathbb C^*$. The same holds for $G$. The gcd estimate therefore gives
\[
                    W=d_F+d_G-o(d_F+d_G).                       \tag{VII.3}
\]
This is where potentially large resonant common factors are accounted for. Relations of bounded length are removed on the formal torus first. There is no assumption that the initially supplied numerator and denominator were close to primitive.

**PROVED (one exceptional set).** On a tail we have $4h>d_F+d_G$. Fix **one** weight vector $n_*$ from this tail, with contact $h_*$ and widths $d_F^*,d_G^*$. Form the finite-type parameter space of coefficient lists with the frozen supports, including those of $F_i-F_1$, and impose $F_iG_i=F_1G_1$ as Laurent polynomial identities. It is defined over $\mathbb Q$. Include all variable scalings $Z\mapsto\lambda Z$ with $\lambda\in(\mathbb C^*)^r$. For every member evaluate at $Z=\lambda t^{n_*}$. The degrees of its two projective maps sum to at most $d_F^*+d_G^*$. The contact is at least $h_*$ because the highest exponent in $F_1$ and those in each $F_i-F_1$ are read from the frozen supports with distinct weights, and scaling a monomial by a nonzero scalar preserves them. By (VII.2) no component of this family dominates $T$. Its evaluation image closure is therefore a proper $\mathbb Q$-closed set.

At $t=1$, $Z=\lambda$ ranges over the entire formal torus. In particular, for the coefficients of **every** term of the subsequence, this same image contains the ratio values at $Z=u^{n_l}$ for all ordinary $u$ where the lists are defined and the values lie in $T$. Closure gives the whole ratio curves. This contradicts the choice of sequence. A separate fixed-degree exceptional set for every exponent vector would not have yielded this conclusion. □

**PROVED (what is missing).** A counterexample escaping fixed closed sets must, for every fixed term budget, escape the class of these quotient presentations on a Zariski-dense tail in the sense of the theorem. Arbitrary rational functions of a given degree need not admit bounded-term quotient presentations. For instance, already the generic polynomial of degree $d$ does not admit such a presentation when $d+1>2b$. For each exponent pattern the locus of coefficients has dimension at most $2b$, and there are countably many patterns over the uncountable field $\mathbb C$. The norm and contact conditions could conceivably force another representation, but I have no such result. Conversely I have no lower bound on contact loss for high-complexity curves outside a fixed closed set.

## VII.3. An obstruction from two real source points

**PROVED.** In the genuine real-structure model, take any Euclidean circle in the source plane whose center is real. It can be chosen separately for each parent. Let \(r\) be half the number of roots of \(N\) off this circle, counted with multiplicity. For five distinct primitive marks,
\[
                 20h\ \le\ 9D+18r.                              \tag{VII.4}
\]
In particular \(h\ge(1/2+\delta)D\) requires \(r/D\ge1/18+10\delta/9\) for **every** such circle. There is no sparsity hypothesis. If all roots lie on such a circle, even three distinct marks require \(2h\le D\). A line is not included in this assertion.

*Proof.* First take a triple and remove its common polynomial gcd. Denote its degree by \(d\), its off-circle degree per mark by \(u\), and its three normalized polynomials by \(F_i\). The contact of the triple is at least the original \(h\), since the ratios do not change. Use a real affine source coordinate \(x\) making the circle the unit circle, and scale by a common constant to make the marks monic. For a polynomial \(F\) of degree \(d\) write \(F^*(x)=x^d\bar F(1/x)\). All \(F_i^*\) agree modulo \(x^h\), with common nonzero value at zero. Factor \(F_i=K_i U_i\) into its on-circle and off-circle parts, both monic. The degree of \(U_i\) is the same \(u\) for all \(i\), because conjugation preserves the circle and \(U_i\bar U_i=N_{\mathrm{off}}\) is independent of \(i\). Likewise \(K_i/K_i^*\) is a constant \(c_i\) of modulus one.

Write \(H_i=F_i/F_i^*=c_i U_i/U_i^*\). The projective point \(p=[H_1:H_2:H_3]\) has degree at most \(2u\) since the three polynomials \(c_i U_i(N_{\mathrm{off}}^*/U_i^*)\) have degrees at most \(2u\). Stars here use the degree of the polynomial before reversal, so this remains true at a zero root. Put \(f=[F_1:F_2:F_3]\) and \(e=[1:1:1]\). The determinant with three rows \(e,p,f\), in basepoint-free homogeneous coordinates for \(p,f\), is a section of degree at most \(d+2u\). It vanishes to order at least \(h\) at infinity because \(f=e+O(x^{-h})\) in projective coordinates. At zero all \(H_i\) are regular and at least one is nonzero by primitivity. Since \(F_i=H_i F_i^*\), the rows for \(f,p\) agree projectively to order at least \(h\) there. Consequently \(2h>d+2u\) makes the determinant identically zero.

Here is why the vanishing is impossible under the strict inequality. If the \(H_i\) are not all equal, generically there are rational functions \(A,B\) with \(F_i=A+B H_i\). Distinctness of the \(F_i\) forces distinct \(H_i\) and \(B\ne0\). At ordinary real \(x\ne0\), both the \(F_i(x)\) and, separately, the \(H_i(x)\) have common modulus, the latter by evaluating the common norm also at \(1/x\). The three distinct \(F_i(x)\) lie on circles centered at zero and at \(A(x)\). A circle through three distinct points is unique. Hence \(A=0\) and \(F_i^*=B\) for all \(i\), an impossibility. If all \(H_i\) are equal, \(f=[F_1^*:F_2^*:F_3^*]\) also has contact at least \(h\) to \(e\) at zero. The sections \(F_i-F_j\) of degree \(d\) in homogeneous coordinates then vanish to order at least \(h\) at both zero and infinity. This forces equality when \(2h>d\). We have proved \(2h\le d+2u\) for each triple.

For the sum over triples one must use their **reduced** degrees. In each conjugate root pair choose an orientation and list, over the five marks, the exponents at its chosen root. Primitivity makes their minimum zero and maximum the parent multiplicity, since the exponents at the conjugate root are complementary. Each threshold between minimum and maximum contributes one to \(D\). Restricting to a triple retains just the thresholds separating that triple, by subtracting the common gcd at both roots. A nontrivial \(2|3\) cut separates nine of the ten triples, a \(1|4\) cut separates six. The same counts apply to the off-circle layers. Hence \(\sum d\le9D\), \(\sum u\le9r\), proving (VII.4). This argument detects the second contact supplied by reflection. A general parent need not have substantial mass near any such symmetry, in the exact root-counting sense used here. \(\square\)

## VII.4. Which linear dependences can survive?

**PROVED.** For \(2h>D\), the space of constant linear relations on the five \(z_i\) is stable under coefficient conjugation. Every four of the \(z_i\) are linearly independent over \(\mathbb C\). Thus a relation, if present, has full support and is unique up to scalar, with a real representative. Such a tuple has **only \(2|3\) layers**, at every degree.

*Proof.* If \(\sum a_i q_i=0\), then \(\sum a_i=0\) and \(\sum\bar a_i/q_i=0\). Since \(1/q_i=2-q_i+O(t^{-2h})\), we have \(\sum\bar a_i z_i=O(t^{D-2h})\). It is a polynomial, hence zero. The same argument applies to any subset. A constant dependence on three distinct marks is impossible for positive contact. A two-term relation would identify a normalized pair. In a genuine three-term relation, dividing by one of the marks gives a unit-circle point constrained to the intersection of two distinct circles with different centers at real source values. Its ratio is constant and hence equal to one.

Suppose four marks were dependent. Their relation \(a\) is unique, has four nonzero entries, and may now be taken real. In four projective coordinates they lie on the plane \(\sum a_i X_i=0\) and, on the torus, on \(\sum a_i/X_i=0\). In the affine chart \(X_1=1\), put \(X_i=1+u_i\) and \(u_1=0\). On the plane \(\sum a_i u_i=0\), the reciprocal equation has quadratic initial term \(\sum a_i u_i^2\), nondegenerate in the two tangent variables. In fact the ambient diagonal form on four variables is nondegenerate, the hyperplane is orthogonal to the all-ones vector, and its radical there is exactly that vector. Taking \(u_1=0\) removes it. Thus the plane curve has an ordinary node at \(e\) with two smooth branches. Explicitly, after a linear coordinate change the quadratic is \(xy\). Blowing up separates the two simple roots of the tangent quadratic, and the implicit function theorem gives each branch as a graph with zero linear error from its tangent. Along the branch used by our parametrization its tangent-line equation therefore vanishes to at least order \(2h\). Multiplying the linear equation in the \(q_i\) by the anchor gives a polynomial of degree at most \(D\), so it vanishes identically. This is an additional independent linear relation on the four marks, contradicting the three-mark assertion. Finally a full-support linear equation cannot vanish at a place where one coordinate alone has minimal order. Its reciprocal equation gives the same rule for the maximal order. A singleton threshold in the five exponents would make one of these extremes unique. \(\square\)

**PROVED.** There is a characteristic-zero ramification test in the remaining real-relation case. Let \(a\) be that real relation. Either the anchored ratios are multiplicatively dependent or at least \(3h+1\) distinct finite geometric places satisfy \(\sum a_i v(q_i)\ne0\). Indeed let \(\ell_i=\log q_i\) formally at infinity. Subtracting the two linear relations gives \(\sum a_i(\exp\ell_i-\exp(-\ell_i))=0\), so \(\sum a_i\ell_i=O(t^{-3h})\). The one-form \(\omega=\sum a_i\,dq_i/q_i\) has a zero of order at least \(3h-1\) at infinity and only simple poles, with the stated residues. Its divisor has degree \(-2\) if it is nonzero. If it is zero, the integral matrix of finite valuations of the four anchored ratios has a nonzero real kernel vector \((a_2,\ldots,a_5)\), hence an integral one. The resulting character has constant value one by evaluation at infinity. At squarefree parents the support can have size \(2D\), so this test does not exclude the endpoint. Independence of all five coordinates as polynomials is also possible. The test is not a general five-point bound.

## VII.5. Four marks, three marks, and five just below the threshold

**PROVED (the common Pell matrix).** Use
\[
 A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t^2+1&t\\t&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \beta=t+2i,\qquad g_0=\beta/\bar\beta .
\]
Choose \(r\) real rational numbers \(s_j\), independently, and let \(G_j=X_j+iY_j\) for \((X_j,Y_j)^t=A(t)^n(1,s_j)^t\), \(n\ge1\). Put \(g_j=G_j/\bar G_j\). Each \(G_j\) has degree \(2n\). For each chosen edge \(jk\) use the following polynomial mark, together with the anchor:
\[
 z_0=\beta\prod_l\bar G_l,\qquad
 z_{jk}=\bar\beta\,G_jG_k\!\prod_{l\ne j,k}\bar G_l,\qquad
 z_{jk}/z_0=g_jg_k/g_0 .
\]
The norms coincide. For four marks use \(r=3\) and edges \(12,13,23\). For five use \(r=4\) and edges \(12,13,23,14\).

For the expansion set \(v=(\sqrt{t^2+4}-t)/2=t^{-1}+O(t^{-3})\) at positive real infinity. The orthogonal eigenvectors are \((1,v),(-v,1)\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda=t^2+O(1)\) and \(\lambda^{-1}\). The phase \(g=(1+iv)/(1-iv)\) satisfies \(g^2=g_0\), since \(1-v^2=tv\). The ratio of the second eigenvector coefficient to the first after powering is \(\lambda^{-2n}(s_j-v)/(1+s_jv)\). This gives
\[
      g_j/g=1+2i s_j t^{-4n}+O(t^{-4n-1}).
\]
Thus \(h=4n\) when at least one chosen edge has \(s_j+s_k\ne0\). The degree is \(D=2rn+1\) for the primitive tuple at generic seeds. Indeed \(G_j,\bar G_j\) are relatively prime by unimodularity. For any root of a previously chosen block or of \(\beta\bar\beta\), vanishing of either orientation of the next block is at most one linear condition on its seed, since \(A(t)^n\) is invertible there. We can avoid all these conditions successively, and the resulting resultant conditions give an open set. Every block has each orientation absent from some mark. Thus the full gcd is one.

For every fixed \(n\), evaluate at \(t=1\). The map from a real seed line to the phase \(g_j(1)\) has dense image on the algebraic one-torus, by invertibility of \(A(1)^n\) and the fractional linear phase map. Independent seeds give the product torus, also after restricting to the indicated nonempty open set or to rational seeds there. The unsigned edge matrix of the triangle has determinant of absolute value two. Appending edge \(14\) and column 4 preserves its full rank. Consequently the edge-product map is dominant, and both families have Zariski-dense ratios. The degree-one correction \(\beta\) removes the square of the **moving** eigenphase, including all its lower Laurent terms.

At five marks \(D=8n+1\), \(h=(D-1)/2\). Inequality (VII.2) implies \(h\le\lfloor(2D-1)/4\rfloor=(D-1)/2\) for a dominating family of this odd degree. At four marks \(D=6n+1\), hence every fixed \(\delta<1/6\) is attained densely for all sufficiently large \(n\).

**PROVED (the inclusive four-point endpoint).** For any genuine pair let \(s_{ij}=\deg(q_i/q_j)\), equal to the sum of the degrees of threshold layers separating the pair. This follows by counting one zero and one pole across the two conjugate roots of each separating layer. Since the ratio is nonconstant, \(h\le s_{ij}\). For \(k=3,4,5\) marks a layer separating \(a\) from \(k-a\) contributes \(a(k-a)\) to the sum of the pair degrees. Thus \(h/D\le2/3,2/3,3/5\), respectively, giving the requested five-point Liouville threshold.

At four marks \(h=2D/3\) forces all layers to be \(2|2\) and each pair degree to equal \(h\). At each root pair all nested nontrivial thresholds are consequently of the same type. Group them into the three \(2|2\) partitions, and denote their total degrees by \(d_j\). Each pair separates two of the partitions, so \(d_1=d_2=d_3=D/3\). Choosing the side of each partition away from the anchor, the three anchored ratios have the form \(g_1g_2,g_1g_3,g_2g_3\) after normalizing all leading constants to one. Here each \(g_j\) is a nonconstant rational function of degree \(d_j\). The three pair products equal \(1+O(t^{-h})\). Taking products and quotients gives \(g_j^2=1+O(t^{-h})\) for each \(j\). Since \(g_j(\infty)=1\) and the characteristic is zero this gives contact at least \(h\) for \(g_j\) itself. But \(d_j<h\). This excludes equality, even for nonsquarefree parents. Hence in the real-structure four-point problem the essential statement fails for every \(\delta<1/6\) and holds vacuously at and above \(1/6\).

**PROVED (three marks).** Let \(G_j=t^d-a_j+i\), \(j=1,2,3\), with distinct rational \(a_j\), and take \(z_j=G_j\prod_{l\ne j}\bar G_l\). The six blocks have disjoint zero sets, so the degree is primitively \(3d\). The phases \(G_j/\bar G_j=1+2it^{-d}+(2ia_j-2)t^{-2d}+O(t^{-3d})\) show that all pair contacts are exactly \(2d\). At \(t=1\) the three independent seeds give three independent phases before division by a common phase, hence dense anchored pairs. Thus for three marks the bound fails densely also at \(\delta=1/6\), and is vacuous above it.

**COMPUTED.** The exact polynomial check over \(\mathbb Q(i)\) verifies common norm, primitive gcd, distinctness, \(D\), and \(h\) for both Pell families at \(n=1,2,3,5\), and for the three-mark family at \(d=1,3,6\). For example the primitive five-mark pairs \((D,h)\) are \((9,4),(17,8),(25,12),(41,20)\). [Reproducible polynomial checks](vii_pell_check.py)

## VII.6. Why the sparse result separates characteristic zero from characteristic two

**PROVED.** The characteristic-two construction gives a sharper comparison than the failure of unrestricted non-density. Over the fixed extension \(\mathbb F_4(t)/\mathbb F_2(t)\), there is a **single finite term budget** \(b\) for sequences of genuine five-tuples that are Zariski dense for every \(\delta<1/30\). Thus the theorem of VII.2 itself fails in characteristic two. I give the density and sparsity details because failure of generic smoothness alone would not show that the two theorems make different predictions on the same class.

Take five elements \(x_j\) linearly independent over \(\mathbb F_2\), initially in an algebraic closure of a rational function field. Write \(V=\sum\mathbb F_2x_j\), \(c=\sum x_j\), and let \(H_j\) be spanned by the four \(x_k\) with \(k\ne j\). Form
\[
 P_j(Y)=\prod_{v\in H_j}(Y+v),\qquad F_j(Y)=P_j(Y)/Y.
\]
The two sides \(H_j,c+H_j\) partition \(V\), so \(F_j(Y)F_j(Y+c)=P_V(Y)/(Y(Y+c))\), independent of \(j\). Each \(P_j\) is additive, since adjoining a generator \(v\) to a subspace \(H\) replaces \(P_H\) by \(P_H^2+P_H(v)P_H\). The common top term \(Y^{16}\) therefore leaves \(\deg_Y(F_i-F_j)\le7\), against \(\deg_Y F_i=15\). With constant independent parameters this already gives \(h/D\ge8/15\) in the split model, and its full polynomial gcd is one since the intersection of the five subspaces is zero.

To see dominance, set \(Y=0\). The five values are the five \(4\times4\) minors of the matrix \((x_j^{2^a})_{0\le a<4,\ 1\le j\le5}\). In four variables the determinant is exactly the product of their fifteen nonzero binary linear forms. Each form divides it by column dependence on its hyperplane, the product has degree fifteen, and the determinant has a monomial with coefficient one. This identifies the scalar over the prime field. Call the vector of minors \(y\). Its entries are linearly independent over \(\mathbb F_2\). Indeed a nonzero binary linear combination is the determinant with a nonzero binary last row adjoined. An invertible binary column transformation takes that row to the last coordinate vector, leaving a nonzero four-variable Moore determinant in four independent transformed \(x_j\). Expansion with a duplicated row gives \(\sum_j y_j x_j^{2^a}=0\) for \(0\le a<4\). Over an algebraically closed field these are the four *linear* equations \(\sum_j y_j^{\,2^{-a}} x_j=0\) after inverse Frobenius. Their rank is four, as seen by raising the matrix to the eighth power, reversing its rows and using the same determinant formula. Thus \(y\) determines the projective point \([x]\) uniquely. The minors define a generically finite map from an open subset of \(\mathbb P^4\), hence dominate \(\mathbb P^4\). We may impose \(c=1\) by scaling the source representatives because all the minors have the same degree. This proves dominance of the anchored ratio map, already on the slice \(Y=0\). Over algebraically closed constants in the split model there are already at most five monomials per mark, since \(P_j\) is linearized of degree sixteen. Replacing \(Y\) by \(t^k\) gives arbitrarily large degrees with this same five-term bound and the same image curves.

Now impose \(c=1\), take \(x_j=t^{n_j}\) for \(1\le j\le4\), \(x_5=1+\sum_{j\le4}x_j\), and \(Y=t^L+\theta\), where \(\theta^2+\theta+1=0\). The nontrivial constant conjugation sends \(Y\) to \(Y+1\). Distinct positive \(n_j\) make the five \(x_j\) independent by comparison of monomials. Write \(B=\max n_j\), and let \(L\) grow as large as needed after the \(n_j\) are fixed. The five univariate \(F_j(Y)\) have degree \(15L\) for \(L>B\). Every coefficient of \(F_i-F_j\) as a polynomial in \(Y\) has \(t\)-degree at most \(16B\). Thus
\[
                       h\ \ge\ 8L-16B .
\]
Distinctness holds for large \(L\), since the differences are nonzero polynomials in \(Y\) over \(\mathbb F_4(t)\). Removing the common polynomial gcd leaves the ratios and their contact unchanged, gives primitive degree \(D\le15L\), and \(D\ge h\) by the degree of any nonconstant pair ratio. Each raw mark is a product of fifteen polynomials of at most six monomials, giving \(b=6^{15}\) for both numerator and denominator of every anchored ratio regardless of the gcd or \(L,n_j\).

For any nonzero equation on the ratio torus, even over \(\overline{\mathbb F_2(t)}\), pull it back to \(x_1,\ldots,x_4,Y\) on \(c=1\). The result is a nonzero rational function by dominance at \(Y=0\). Independently varying \(t^{n_1},\ldots,t^{n_4}\) is Zariski dense over that field, as each coordinate set is infinite. Avoiding the diagonals, choose the four exponents to leave a nonzero rational function of \(Y\), then choose arbitrarily large \(L\) avoiding its finitely many zeros and poles. This verifies the strict \(\delta<1/30\) statement over the fixed field, with the same \(b\) throughout. It is an application of the other thread's subspace-polynomial construction to the sparse class. [Binary norm construction, Part V](../free/answer.md)

**PROVED (the failing step).** In the Moore-minor evaluation, differentiating the three kernel equations with \(a=1,2,3\) gives \(\sum_j x_j^{2^a}\,dy_j=0\) because the derivatives of the \(x_j^{2^a}\) vanish. The three constraint rows are independent. They leave a two-dimensional vector space of infinitesimal variations containing the radial direction. The projective differential therefore has rank at most one, although the image has dimension four. This is exactly the implication from dominance to full differential rank needed in (VII.2). The finite-field drop in degrees comes from the same Frobenius in the additive polynomial. In the ternary construction the identity \(P_{H+\mathbb F_q v}=P_H^q-P_H(v)^{q-1}P_H\) gives the corresponding degree drop for the reflection construction. For example the binomial identity making \(X^q\) additive fails in characteristic zero because its derivative is nonconstant for \(q>1\). I make no term-budget assertion here for the fixed ternary examples. [Ternary construction, VI.4 of the parallel report](../free/answer.md)

## VII.7. Arithmetic scope and the outstanding five-point step

**PROVED.** The sparse proof has a two-place version useful for comparing it to polynomial Pell presentations. If each numerator and denominator is a sum of at most \(b\) Chebyshev polynomials \(T_j(at+b_0)\) with arbitrary indices and coefficients, the conclusion of VII.2 still holds. Here the affine coordinate is common. Pull back by \(at+b_0=(u+u^{-1})/2\), so \(2T_j=u^j+u^{-j}\). The pullback has height \(2W\) and contact \(h\) at both \(0\) and \(\infty\). Apply the proof of VII.2 keeping **both** weight extremes of the same supports of \(F_i-F_1\) relative to \(F_1\). Use the same gcd estimate and coefficient family. At zero the determinant in (VII.2) has zero of order at least \(4h-1\), and at infinity at least \(4h+1\). Neither contributes a boundary pole, so a dominating frozen family would require raw height at least \(8h\), whereas (VII.3) gives raw height \(2W+o(W)<8h\). The pullback has the same image. Thus the growing exponents and a large primitive cancellation together do not circumvent the theorem in this trace family.

**PROVED (what transfers).** The sparse gcd step counts roots of a known nonzero resultant in one variable. Its elementary integer counterpart is divisibility by the value of a resultant after coefficient denominators and powers of the base are accounted for. It does not supply four independent derivatives for integer points. The geometric step uses a family of maps and the degree of a rational function on its source. In VI.1 the arithmetic input was a number-field Subspace Theorem with a fixed finite set of places. Neither proof gives a moving-place five-mark theorem for integers. In Theorem 4(i) of Guo–Nguyen–Sun–Wang, an allowed alternative for a fixed constant polynomial bounds the maximum unit height by \(c_0\max(1,2g-2+|S|)\). On \(\mathbb P^1\) with \(|S|\) comparable to \(D\), that alternative need not contradict the five-point height, so one cannot simply invoke the small-error alternative 4(ii). VII.2 instead puts the *input monomials* on the fixed two-place torus and controls the height loss in their images. [Guo, Nguyen, Sun and Wang on Vojta's generalized \(abc\) for tori](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.15881)

**PROVED (specialization with slack).** If ratio curves over \(\mathbb Q(i)[t]\) were dense with \(h/D\ge1/2+\delta_0\), their integer specializations would refute \(A_5(\delta)\) for every \(0<\delta<\delta_0\). For a prescribed proper closed subset choose a curve escaping it. Clear a common coefficient denominator. A polynomial Bézout identity for the primitive \(z_i\), after clearing its Gaussian rational coefficients, shows that their specialized Gaussian gcd divides a fixed nonzero Gaussian integer. Thus the primitive radius at large positive integers \(T\) is \(\asymp_f T^D\), and the normalized width is \(O_f(T^{-h})\). The ratio points are outside the prescribed set for all sufficiently large \(T\), because some defining equation pulls back to a nonzero rational function. Strict slack absorbs all curve-dependent constants. Examples over \(\mathbb C[t]\) alone give no such arithmetic specialization. The five-mark family of VII.5 approaches \(h/D=1/2\) from below, so supplies no positive \(\delta\) by this argument.

**OPEN (research verdict).** The missing step is a uniform exceptional set, or a counterexample, for maps of unbounded presentation complexity on unrestricted root supports. In the sparse proof the resultant has a bounded support box for each chosen Dirichlet accuracy, and a single coefficient space can then be used for every remaining exponent vector. Both controls disappear when the number of terms grows with the height. The two-real-point estimate bypasses them only when there is a second, sufficiently inexpensive approximation. The \(24\)-mark theorem and the exclusion of dominating families of fixed degree are consistent with either answer to the unrestricted five-point question.
