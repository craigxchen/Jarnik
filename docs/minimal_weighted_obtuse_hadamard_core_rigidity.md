# Minimal weighted-obtuse sign simplices: fair cuts and core rigidity

Let `S` have `M=r+1` distinct rows in `{+1,-1}^r`, and let every coordinate
have a positive weight `w_j`. Assume all off-diagonal entries of
`G=S diag(w) S^t` are strictly negative. These are inner products of the
vectors from the origin, not dihedral angles of the simplex. The following
necessary conditions use arbitrary positive weights; they do not classify
all such sign matrices.

## A common positive row mass bisects every sign cut

The row rank of `S` is `M-1`, and its left kernel is generated by a vector
`p` with every coordinate strictly positive. Indeed, the `M` rows in
`R^(M-1)` have a nonzero linear relation. If a relation had both positive
and negative coefficients, moving its negative part to the other side
would express the same vector as two positive sums over disjoint row sets.
Their mutual inner product would be strictly negative, contradicting its
nonnegative squared norm. Thus every nonzero relation is one-signed. Two
linearly independent relations can be combined into a mixed-sign relation,
so the kernel is one-dimensional. A nonnegative relation cannot have a zero
coordinate: taking its inner product with an omitted row would give zero on
one side and a strictly negative number on the other. Hence `p_i>0`.

Since `S` is integral, `p` can be chosen as a primitive positive integer
vector. Every column `j` obeys

```text
sum_(i:s_ij=+1) p_i = sum_(i:s_ij=-1) p_i = (sum_i p_i)/2.    (1)
```

Consequently each present cut must bisect the *same* integral row mass.
If that mass is uniform, `M` must be even and every column has exactly
`M/2` signs of each kind. For a nonuniform mass, (1) restricts which cuts
can appear, but it does not by itself force uniformity.

## Difference supports must intersect

For a row pair `e={i,k}`, write `D_e={j:s_ij!=s_kj}` and let
`d_w(e)=sum_(j in D_e)w_j` be its weighted Hamming distance.
Because the common squared row norm is `W=sum_j w_j`, strict negative
Gram product is equivalent to `d_w(e)>W/2`. If two pair supports
`D_e,D_f` were disjoint, then `d_w(e)+d_w(f)<=W`, contradicting both
strict inequalities. Therefore

```text
D_e intersect D_f != empty for all distinct row pairs e,f.  (2)
```

When `e={i,k}` and `f={i,l}` share a row, (2) says some coordinate
has `s_ij` opposite to both `s_kj` and `s_lj`; equivalently row `i`
cannot lie in the coordinate box between rows `k,l`. Condition (2)
also constrains disjoint row pairs, so it is stronger than that triple
condition. It requires no kernel calculation and survives arbitrary
positive coordinate weights.

An exact finite enumeration of anchored sign matrices gives a small-order
consequence: no `5 x 4` or `6 x 5` sign matrix has all its pair supports
intersecting. Fixing the first row positive in every column is harmless,
and each distinct unoriented nonconstant cut is represented by one
mask containing that first row. The checker enumerates all choices of
`M-1` such columns, discards repeated rows, and tests (2) using integer
bit masks. Removing repeated or constant columns preserves every support
intersection; adding unused distinct nonconstant columns then preserves
it as well. Thus this enumeration also excludes matrices that initially
have repeated or constant columns. It finds one eligible `4 x 3` matrix (the Hadamard simplex),
zero among 1015 distinct-row `5 x 4` matrices, and zero among 126651
distinct-row `6 x 5` matrices. Thus strict weighted obtuseness is
impossible at `M=5,6`, with no restriction on the positive weights.
The filter alone does not prove Hadamard rigidity at larger orders:
the eight binary rows encoded by `0,7,25,30,42,45,51,52` in seven
coordinates have pairwise-intersecting difference supports, but their
ordinary pair distances include both three and four. Their first seven
sign rows have determinant `512`, so this near miss has full column rank.
It is a combinatorial near miss, not an obtuse weighted witness.

## Rigidity after retaining all but one Hadamard cut

Suppose `M` is a Hadamard order and `H=[1,h_1,...,h_(M-1)]` is a normalized
`M x M` Hadamard matrix. Fix one nonconstant column `chi`, retain the other
`M-2` nonconstant columns, and take one arbitrary sign column `f`. If
`S=[H_without_1_and_chi,f]` is strictly weighted-obtuse for *any* positive
weights on all its columns, then

```text
f=chi or f=-chi.                                             (3)
```

Proof. Partition the rows into `A={i:chi_i=+1}` and
`B={i:chi_i=-1}`. Each retained column `h` has zero sum separately on
`A` and `B`: this follows from `h·1=0` and `h·chi=0`. Fix `x in A`
and sum the negative Gram entries `G_xy` over `y in B`. The retained
columns cancel regardless of their weights, leaving

```text
sum_(y in B) G_xy = w_f f_x sum_(y in B) f_y < 0.           (4)
```

The sum on `B` is nonzero, and (4) forces every `f_x` on `A` to be
the same sign. Summing instead over `x in A` for each fixed `y in B`
forces `f` to be constant on `B`, with the opposite sign. This proves
(3). In particular an arbitrary one-column replacement of an exact
Hadamard core cannot preserve strict origin-pairwise obtuseness, even
after freely reweighting every retained column.

This conclusion is sharp: taking `f=±chi` and equal weights gives
`G_ik=-1` for all `i!=k` after deleting the all-ones column.

## A nonuniform seven-row mass excluded by a graph count

There is no strictly weighted-obtuse `7 x 6` sign matrix whose primitive
positive kernel mass, up to row permutation, is `(2,1,1,1,1,1,1)`.
Call the mass-two row `h` and the six mass-one rows `1,...,6`.
Every fair sign cut from (1) has the heavy row and exactly two light
rows on one side. Orient its column to make `h` positive. Thus the six
columns of `S` are edges of a simple graph on the six light rows;
the full column rank from the first theorem forbids duplicate or opposite
columns, so these are distinct edges. Write `W` for their total weight,
`d_i` for the weighted degree of light vertex `i`, and `w_ik` for the
weight of edge `ik` when present, zero otherwise. Weighted Hamming
distances are

```text
D(h,i)=W-d_i,
D(i,k)=d_i+d_k-2w_ik.                              (5)
```

Negative Gram product is exactly `D(i,k)>W/2`. The heavy-light pairs
therefore give `d_i<W/2`. A light vertex of graph degree zero has
`D(i,k)=d_k<W/2` against any other light vertex. If `i` has graph
degree one, with sole edge `ik`, then `D(i,k)=d_k-w_ik<W/2`.
Thus every light vertex has graph degree at least two. Six edges have
total graph degree twelve, forcing every vertex to have graph degree
exactly two. In this two-regular graph, each edge touches exactly two
other edges. Sum the required inequalities `D(i,k)>W/2` over its six
selected edges `ik`: by (5), every column weight appears twice on the
left, yielding `2W>3W`, a contradiction. The proof permits arbitrary
positive edge weights.
